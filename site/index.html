Volens Field Manual

A bot that watches wallets which have made money, buys what they buy, and sells on rules you set. Everything is driven from Telegram buttons. This is how to run it without hurting yourself.

01What it actually does

The whole idea in four sentences.

Volens listens to every Solana transaction on the exchanges where new tokens trade — tens of thousands a minute. It keeps a list of 1,117 tracked wallets that have historically traded well, of which about 780 are in the active cohort.

When several of those wallets independently buy the same token inside a ten-minute window, that's a signal. If the signal clears your thresholds and every safety check passes, the bot buys — then watches the position and sells it on a ladder you define.

It does not predict anything. It follows people, quickly.

02The one control that matters

Learn this before anything else.

HALT stops all trading instantly. Not the next cycle — immediately, and it's checked before every single buy and every single sell.

Telegram
/halt — stop everything /resume — start again

It writes a file to disk, which means it survives a restart. If the machine reboots while halted, it comes back halted. That's deliberate: the safe state has to be the sticky one.

If anything looks wrong

Hit /halt first and ask questions after. There is no cost to halting and no cost to resuming. Every other control on this page is a preference; this one is a brake.

04What triggers a buy

Smart-money SOL volume — not how many wallets bought.

The bot sums what the tracked cohort spent on one token inside a ten-minute window. When that total crosses your threshold, it buys.

🟢 On — tap to disable
💸 Smart volume · 10 SOL ◀️ Back

Size is the signal, not headcount. Five wallets putting in 0.05 SOL each is not the same conviction as two putting in 5 SOL, and a count cannot tell them apart. There is no wallet-count setting — it was removed, not merely defaulted off.

Choosing the number

Measured across 91,672 tracked buys and 11,064 tokens:

The median tracked buy is 0.95 SOL, and the median token attracts 3.1 SOL in its best ten minutes. So anything under 2 SOL is close to no filter at all.

Threshold and cap do different jobs

The threshold controls quality; trades-per-day controls quantity. Even at 20% selectivity, thousands of tokens flow through daily — so you need both.

How an order works

Every order is two numbers: a trigger and how much to sell.

  • Negative trigger = a stop. -25% sells when the position is 25% below cost. Set its amount to 100% unless you have a reason not to.
  • Positive trigger = a target. +100% sells when the position has doubled.
  • Amounts are shares of the original position, so they add up. Targets totalling more than 100% means the later ones find less than they asked for — the screen shows the running total and flags it.

A worked ladder: take your stake back at the first double, then let the rest run.

-25% → sell 100% the stop +100% → sell 50% initial stake back +250% → sell 20% +400% → sell 20% +900% → sell 10% the runner
  • Stops are checked before targets. A position that gaps from +300% to −60% exits rather than taking a target on the way past.
  • Targets fire lowest-first, once each.
  • Break-even is a toggle, not a level. Once a position has been up, if it comes back it exits at cost — no profit, no loss.
  • Trailing sells everything once the position falls a set percentage from its peak, and only after it has been in profit.
Do not set the stop too tight

You buy at the ask and the exit prices at the bid. On a fresh memecoin that gap alone is several percent, so a −5% stop trips before the token has moved at all. This is not theoretical: in one live session, five of seven positions stopped out within 26 seconds of entry, purely on the spread. Keep the stop outside the round-trip cost — −25% or wider at small trade sizes.

06Alpha — size, not signal

Alpha does not find trades. It decides that a trade auto-buy already wants is worth more money.

The bot scores every tracked wallet on how its past calls actually performed. When auto-buy fires on a token, Alpha asks one question: is a wallet with a proven record among the buyers? If so, it adds a fixed amount on top of the size already chosen. If not, the trade executes at the normal size.

Add-on 0.02 SOL
Alpha · 🟢 On
💰 Add-on · 0.02 SOL ◀️ Back

Two controls, and that is the whole mode. There is no separate trigger, no separate exits and no separate position limit — because there is no separate position. One entry, one ladder, one set of limits.

Order matters

The add-on is applied after the market-cap band picks the base size. Adding first would inflate the valuation estimate and let conviction quietly move a token into a different band. Every limit afterwards — ceiling, affordability, price impact, supply share, daily cap, max open — measures the combined total, because that is what leaves the wallet.

If the add-on would breach the per-trade ceiling, or you cannot afford it, the trade still executes at the base size. The add-on is the discretionary half, so it is what gives way — refusing a trade the normal rules already approved because of an optional extra would be the wrong way round.

Which wallets count

Scored from the call history you can already see: which tracked wallets bought each announced token, and the peak that token reached. Nothing extra is recorded, so any score can be reconciled against the leaderboard.

RuleDefault
Resolved calls needed20

Three of those rules exist because of things that went wrong. Hit rate alone promoted a wallet whose median call did 1.00× — half its picks went nowhere, and it cleared the bar anyway. A bar of 35% admitted half the book, because a wallet's rate is only ever measured on tokens that got called, and the median wallet already scores ~33% there. And a call nobody managed to price is not a miss — two thirds of calls were never re-priced at all, so counting them measured the pricing sweep rather than the wallet.

Scoring is continuous. Resolved calls are archived when the tracker retires them, so a wallet's record accumulates over 30 days rather than being rebuilt from whatever is still being tracked. The wallets are re-ranked daily.

These thresholds live in the host config, not Telegram. Which wallets get trusted with money is a decision to make deliberately, not a slider to move between trades.

07Rebound — the tokens that come back

Every token smart money touches is watched for days afterwards. Some of them start trading again.

The other two modes fire while tracked wallets are buying. Rebound watches what happens next. A token enters the watchlist automatically the moment tracked money touches it, and stays there for a configurable window — 72 hours by default. Nothing is bought on entry: the watchlist is a list, not a position.

If the token goes quiet and then starts trading again on fresh volume from anyone at all, you get an alert. If a buy amount is configured, it is also bought — once.

🔄 TRADING AGAIN · PEPE · 7TGAy4G9…pump 14.2 SOL in the last few minutes · watched 31h 87% below where smart money bought

That last line is the point. "A token is trading" is unremarkable; "a token that fell 87% is trading again" is the thing worth looking at. The price is captured when the watch opens, so the drawdown is measured against where the tracked wallets actually were.

It has to leave before it can come back

A token joins the watchlist at the exact moment wallets are buying it, so its volume is at its highest right then. Nothing can fire until the token has been observed below the threshold at least once. Without that rule Rebound would trigger on everything within seconds of adding it, and would just be a slower copy of auto-buy.

Fresh volume, not lifetime volume

The trigger measures volume in a short rolling window, not since the token was first seen. Cumulative volume only ever grows, so a threshold against it fires once on every token that has ever traded and says nothing about whether anything is happening now.

Alerts do not need a buy amount. Turn Rebound on with just a volume threshold and you get the signal feed with no trades — which is how the buy amount should be chosen: from what you actually see, over a few days.

Rebound is genuinely independent: its own trigger, size, exit ladder and position limit. It shares every safety veto, because those are written once. It will not buy a token you already hold — its thesis is a re-entry after the move, not adding to something open.

Alerts are capped at five per pass so a low threshold cannot flood the group; anything not announced is picked up on the next pass rather than lost.

08Sizing by market cap

One SOL amount for every token treats a $20K launch like a $2M one.

📊 Buy by market cap
Default buy: 0.20 SOL $10K–$50K → 0.10 SOL $50K–$100K → 0.20 SOL $100K–$500K → 0.35 SOL $500K–$1M → 0.50 SOL
➕ Add band ♻️ Reset

Adding a band is two taps — pick a range, pick a size. Bands cannot overlap, so every token matches at most one, and a token in no band uses the default. You never have to fill in the whole table.

Separately, Max market cap refuses a token outright. The two are different jobs: the ceiling decides whether, bands decide how much.

Supply cap

A ceiling on how much of a token's supply one position may hold, as a percentage. The buy always executes at its configured size; afterwards the real holding is measured and only the excess is sold.

It matters as trade size grows: on an early token a fixed SOL amount can buy a double-digit share of everything that exists — a position nobody will take back off you. Price impact does not catch this, because impact measures the pool and this measures the token.

Every one of these refuses the trade on its own.

All of them fail closed. If a check can't be read — network down, price missing — it refuses rather than assuming the best. A skipped trade costs nothing; a wrong one costs money.

They run on the total size, including the Alpha add-on, and every mode goes through them. Rebound has its own trigger and exits but not its own copy of these — a parallel path would be a second place for a check to be forgotten.

Max open positions defaults to 1

Concentration is deliberate. A bot that opens a position on every signal it likes spreads the same balance across trades it cannot then size properly, and on a small wallet it runs out of fees before it runs out of signals. Raise it under Limits once you know what you want.

12.9% of signals touch 2× at some point, but only 9% are still there at the end. The median signal peaks at 1.08× — essentially flat — and 70% of what the cohort buys eventually rugs.

That says the edge lives in exits, not entries. A signal that touches 2× and gives it back is a win you had and lost, which is exactly what the stop, break-even and trailing settings exist to keep.

12A safe first session

In order. The whole point is that the last step is boring.

  1. Fund a dedicated wallet

    Never your main one. Enough that fees aren't most of the cost — around 1 SOL is a sensible floor.

  2. Back up the key somewhere else

    If the machine dies or the host suspends the box, an unexported key is simply gone.

  3. Set the trigger

    Smart volume decides everything. 10 SOL admits about the top 20% of tokens; below 2 SOL is barely a filter.

  4. Set the limits before arming

    Trade size and trades/day. Decide what a bad day looks like while you're calm.

  5. Set a stop you'd actually accept

    Add one order under Selling → Orders: trigger −25%, amount 100%. Wide enough to sit outside the spread — −5% will stop you out on entry. Add targets later, once you have watched a few trades.

  6. Leave Alpha and Rebound off for the first session

    One thing at a time. Alpha increases the size of every qualifying trade, and Rebound opens trades of its own — both make a first session harder to read.

  7. Resume, and watch the first trade land

    Buy alert, then sell alert. Confirm the exit reason matches what the token actually did.

  8. Then leave it alone

    Judge it over dozens of trades, not one. A single result tells you nothing either way.

13Honest limits

Things worth knowing before trusting it with size.

  • It follows, it doesn't predict. If the tracked wallets have a bad week, so does the bot.
  • Volume has no direction. A token collapsing prints enormous volume too. Smart-money buying supplies the direction; the token-volume filter only confirms.
  • 70% of what the cohort buys rugs, and the average position ends around half. Following it broadly loses money — the edge is in the exits and in the handful of wallets whose results hold up.
  • Some tokens can't be sold at any price. If liquidity is pulled, no exit strategy helps. The stop can only fire while someone is still buying.
  • Speed is location. Running near the data source is worth more than any code change — the same bot is about 830ms faster per entry on a well-placed server than on home internet.
  • Only ever run one copy. Two instances sharing a wallet will double-trade every signal.
  • Don't share the trading wallet with another app. The bot reconstructs cost basis from its own trade log, so a manual buy or sell made elsewhere from the same wallet is invisible to it — positions and P&L drift until the next reconciliation.
  • Alpha scores from the call, not from each wallet's own entry. Per-wallet fill prices aren't recorded, so a wallet that bought late is credited with a little more than it earned. The sample minimum is set high enough that this can't promote a wallet on its own.
  • Keep enough SOL for fees. Exits cost a fee, and a routed exit can need rent up front. A wallet too empty to pay it cannot sell — the stop fires and is rejected.
  • Token account rent is not returned automatically. Every buy creates a token account holding ~0.00204 SOL of rent. Selling empties it but does not close it, so that SOL stays locked until you reclaim it yourself. At a 0.01 SOL trade size that is a fifth of the position, on every token — it is the main argument for trading larger than that.
